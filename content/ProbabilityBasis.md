## Overview

### Random Variables and Distributions

A *random variable* (often abbreviated as RV) is a quantity whose value is determined by chance. Think of it as the outcome of a random device, like the roll of a die, or a process with inherent variability, like the mood of your cat.

While the outcome of a single trial is unpredictable, repeated observations of a random variable often exhibit stable statistical patterns. This idea is formalized through the notion of a *distribution*.

Suppose we repeat the random experiment $n$ times and observe outcomes $\{x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^n$. We denote the underlying random variable by an uppercase letter $X$, and each observed outcome by lowercase $x^{(i)}$, called a *realization* of $X$.

The *empirical measure* is defined as
$$
\mu_n(A) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{I}(x^{(i)} \in A),
$$
where $A$ is any subset of possible outcomes, and $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is the *indicator function*, which equals 1 if its argument is true and 0 otherwise. This expression gives the fraction of observations that fall within the set $A$.

We typically assume that the observations are generated by repeated, independent trials of the same experiment, which is known as the *i.i.d. assumption* (independent and identically distributed). Under this assumption, it is reasonable to expect the empirical measure to stabilize as the number of trials increases. That is, for each measurable set $A$, the frequency $\mu_n(A)$ should converge to a limiting value:
$$
\mu_X(A) := \mathbb{P}(X\in A) := \lim_{n \to \infty} \mu_n(A).
$$
This limiting function $\mu$, which maps a subset $A$ to a probability scalar in $[0,1]$, 
is a *probability measure*,  or *distribution* of the random variable $X$. It quantifies how likely it is for outcomes to fall within any set $A$. 
It is expected that a probability measure should satisfy the following basic properties: 
$$
\mu(\Omega) =1, ~~~ \mu(A) \geq 0, ~~~ \mu(A\cup B) = \mu(A) + \mu(B)- \mu(A\cap B), 
$$
for any sets $A, B\subseteq \Omega$ on which $\mu$ is well defined (such sets are called measurable). 

More generally, we can define the *expected value* of a function $f(X)$ through its empirical average:
$$
\mathbb{E}[f(X)] = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n f(x^{(i)}) = \int f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_X(x).
$$
Here we write $\int f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_X(x)$ to be the integration of $f$ w.r.t. measure $\mu_X$. 

#### Discrete Random Variables
If $\Omega$ is a finite or countable set, 
we can fully specify the distribution of $X$ 
by *probability mass function* (PMF) $p_X(x)$ that gives the probability that $X$ takes the specific value $x$:
$$
p_X(x) = \mathbb{P}(X = x) \quad \text{for each } x \in \Omega.
$$
The probability of any subset $A\subseteq \Omega$ is obtained by summing:
$$
\mathbb{P}(X \in A) = \sum_{x \in A} p_X(x).
$$
As defined as limits of the empirical frequencies, we should obviously have $\sum_x p_X(x)=1$ and $p_X(x)\geq 0$ for all $x\in \Omega$.  


**Example [Bernoulli and Categorical Distributions].**  
The Bernoulli distribution, denoted as $\mathtt{Bernouli}(p)$, is a discrete distribution on $\{0,1\}$ with probability mass function: 
$$
p_X(x) = \begin{cases}
p & \text{if } x=1, \\
1-p & \text{if } x=0.
\end{cases}
$$
The Categorical distribution is a discrete distribution on $\{1,2,\ldots, k\}$ with probability mass function: 
$$
p_X(x_i) =  p_i,
~~~~~~ \text{where $p_i \geq 0$ and $\sum_{i=1}^k p_i = 1$.} 
$$     
It is denoted as $\mathtt{Categorical}([p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_k])$. 

#### Continuous Random Variables
When $\Omega$ is the Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^d$,
we can characterize the distribution with the *probability density functions* (PDFs), as the limit of probabilities over shrinking regions:
$$
p_X(x) = \lim_{r \to 0^+} \frac{\mathbb{P}(X \in B_r(x))}{\mathrm{Vol}(B_r(x))},
$$
where $B_r(x)$ is a ball of radius $r$ centered at $x$, and $\mathrm{Vol}(B_r(x))$ is its volume. 
This limit may not exist, but if it does, 
the density function $p_X$ provides a full characterization  of distribution of $X$ through integration: 
$$
\mathbb{P}(X \in A) = \int_A p_X(x) \,\mathrm{d}x, \qquad
\mathbb{E}[f(X)] = \int f(x) p_X(x) \,\mathrm{d}x. 
$$
We call that $X$ (and its distribution) absolutely continuous in this case. 
Similar to PMF, we should have $\int p_X(x) \,\mathrm{d}x =1$ and $p_X(x)\geq 0$ for all $x\in \Omega$.  

**Example [Uniform Distribution].**  
The uniform distribution on $[a,b]$ is a continuous distribution on $\mathbb{R}$ with density:
$$
p_X(x) =
\begin{cases}
 \frac{1}{b-a}, ~~~ \text{for } x \in [a,b]\\
 0, ~~~ \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$
It is denoted as $\mathtt{Uniform}([a,b])$. 

**Example [Continuous and Discrete Gaussian Distrbutions].**  
The Gaussian distribution, denoted as $\mathtt{Normal}(\mu,\sigma^2)$ is a continuous distribution on $\mathbb{R}^d$ with density:
$$
p_X(x) = \frac{1}{Z} \exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), 
$$
where $\mu$ is the mean and $\sigma$ is the standard deviation, and $Z$ is a normalization constant ensuring $\int p_X(x) \,\mathrm{d}x =1$:
$$ 
Z := \int \exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \,\mathrm{d}x = \sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}. 
$$
%%
If we restrict the density on the set of integers $\mathbb{Z}$, we get the discrete Gaussian distribution whose probabiilty mass function is: 
$$
p_X(x) = \frac{1}{Z_n} \exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), ~~~~Z_n = \sum_{x\in \mathbb{Z}^d} \exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),
$$
where the normalization constant is different from the continuous case. It does not have a closed formula like the continous case. 

**Example [Hybrid Distributions].**  
Consider the following random variable: 
$$
X = \begin{cases}
    Z \sim \mathtt{Normal}(0,1)& \text{with probability } 0.5 , \\
    Y\sim \mathtt{Bernouli}(0.5) & \text{with probability } 0.5.
\end{cases}
$$
It is a mixture of a continuous and discrete random variables. 

### Ways to Build Distributions

In practice, we often need to construct complex distributions from simpler  distributions. This is fundamental to many areas of machine learning, including generative models, variational inference, and Monte Carlo methods. There are two different construction approaches. 

#### 1. Variable Transformation (Change of Variables)
Given a reference random variable $\omega \sim \mu_0$, we can construct new random variables through deterministic transformations:
$$
X = T(\omega),
$$
where $T: \Omega \to \Omega'$ is a measurable mapping. The resulting random variable $X$ follows a transformed distribution.

**Theorem [Change of Variables Formula].**  
If $T$ is continuously differentiable and invertible, and $\omega$ has density $p_\omega$, then $X = T(\omega)$ has density:
$$
p_X(x) = p_\omega(T^{-1}(x)) \cdot \left|\det(\nabla T^{-1}(x))\right|,
$$
where $\nabla T^{-1}(x)$ is the Jacobian matrix of the inverse transformation.

The factor $\left|\det(\nabla T^{-1}(x))\right|$ accounts for how the transformation changes the volume element.

**Proof.**  
For any measurable function $h$, we have 
$$
\mathbb{E}[h(X)]=\int p_X(x) h(x) \,\mathrm{d}x.
$$
On the other hand, we have 
$$
\mathbb{E}[h(X)] = \mathbb{E}[h(T(\omega))]
= \int h(T(\omega)) p_\omega(\omega) \,\mathrm{d}\omega 
= \int h(x) p_\omega(T^{-1}(x)) \,\mathrm{d} T^{-1}(x) 
= \int h(x) p_\omega(T^{-1}(x)) \left|\det(\nabla T^{-1}(x))\right| \,\mathrm{d}x. 
$$
Matching this with (equ:ehpx), we get $p_X(X) = p_\omega(T^{-1}(X)) \cdot \left|\det(\nabla T^{-1}(X))\right|$. 

**Example [Log-Normal Distribution].**  
Let $\omega \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ be standard normal, and define $X = \exp(\omega)$. Then:
$$
p_X(x) = \frac{1}{x\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left(-\frac{(\log x)^2}{2}\right), \quad x > 0.
$$
This gives the log-normal distribution, useful for modeling positive-valued data like incomes or stock prices.

**Example [Box-Muller Transform].**  
To generate samples from $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$, we can use the Box-Muller transform. Let $U_1, U_2 \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$ be independent, then:
$$
Z_1 = \sqrt{-2\log U_1} \cos(2\pi U_2), \quad Z_2 = \sqrt{-2\log U_1} \sin(2\pi U_2)
$$
are independent $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ random variables.

#### 2. Density Modifications
Instead of transforming the base distribution, we can modify its probability measures or density functions to obtain different distributions. 
One fundamental approach is through reweighting the density functions:
$$
p(x) = \frac{p_\omega(x) \cdot r(x)}{Z}, \quad \text{where } Z = \int p_\omega(x) r(x) \,\mathrm{d}x.
$$
Here $r(x)$ is called the *importance weight function* and $Z$ is the normalization constant that ensures the resulting function integrates to 1.

**Example [Gaussian Distribution with Different Means].**  
Let $\phi(x)$ be the density function of the standard normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(0,I)$. 
Then the density of $\mathcal{N}(\mu, I)$ can be obtained by reweighting:
$$
p(x) = \phi(x) \exp\left(x^\top \mu - \frac{1}{2}\|\mu\|^2\right). 
$$
This demonstrates how a simple exponential reweighting can shift the mean of a distribution while preserving its covariance structure.

**Example [Truncated Normal Distribution].**  
Let $\omega \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and define the truncation weight $r(x) = \mathbb{I}(x \in [a,b])$. Then the truncated distribution has density:
$$
p(x) = \frac{\phi(x) \cdot \mathbb{I}(x \in [a,b])}{\Phi(b) - \Phi(a)},
$$
where $\phi$ and $\Phi$ are the standard normal PDF and CDF respectively. This creates a distribution that is zero outside the interval $[a,b]$ and properly normalized within it.

**Remark [Importance of Normalization].**  
When modifying density functions, it's crucial to ensure the result integrates to 1. The normalization constant $Z$ in reweighting methods serves this purpose. Without proper normalization, the modified function would not be a valid probability density.

Reweighting is not the only way to modify density functions to create new valid distributions. 

**Example [Mixture Distributions].**  
A mixture distribution combines multiple component distributions with weights:
$$
p(x) = \sum_{k=1}^K w_k p_k(x), \quad \text{where } w_k \geq 0, \sum_{k=1}^K w_k = 1.
$$
Each $p_k$ is a valid density function, and the mixture preserves the property that $\int p(x) \,\mathrm{d}x = 1$. Mixtures are particularly useful for modeling multi-modal data or approximating complex distributions.

**Example [Power Transformations of CDFs].**  
Let $p_0$ be a density function, and $F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} p_0(z) \,\mathrm{d}z$ be its cumulative distribution function (CDF). For $\alpha > 0$, we can define a new distribution:
$$
F_\alpha(x) = F(x)^\alpha, \quad \text{and} \quad p_\alpha(x) = \alpha F(x)^{\alpha-1} p_0(x).  
$$
This transformation creates a new density function $p_\alpha$ that concentrates more mass in regions where $F(x)$ is large when $\alpha > 1$, and spreads it out when $0 < \alpha < 1$. The factor $\alpha$ ensures proper normalization.

### Formal Mathematical Framework

#### Probability Measures

Both PMFs and PDFs are density functions for underlying probability measures. A *probability measure* $P$ is a function $P \colon \mathcal{B}(\Omega) \to [0,1]$ that maps measurable subsets $\mathcal{B}(\Omega) \subseteq 2^{\Omega}$ to probabilities, satisfying:

1. $P(\emptyset) = 0$ and $P(\Omega) = 1$
2. For any countable collection of disjoint sets $\{A_i\}$, $P(\cup_i A_i) = \sum_i P(A_i)$

The measure $P$ relates to density functions through:
$$
P(A) = \int_A p(x) \,\mathrm{d}x \quad \text{(continuous)}, \quad P(A) = \sum_{x \in A} p(x) \quad \text{(discrete)}.
$$

#### Random Variables as Functions

Probability measures provide a static description by assigning likelihoods to all possible outcomes. In contrast, random variables offer a dynamic perspective by describing how randomness unfolds through a function. These are two complementary perspectives for describing randomness.

Formally, a random variable $X$ is a measurable function that maps elements from a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ to real numbers:
$$
X \colon \Omega \to \mathbb{R}, \quad \omega \mapsto X(\omega).
$$
When we draw a random seed $\omega$ according to the probability measure $P$, the function $X(\omega)$ transforms this basic source of randomness into the observed random value. This dynamic process shows how complex random phenomena can emerge from simple random seeds.

**Example [Multiple Random Variables on Same Space].**  
Consider the probability space $(\Omega, P)$ where $\Omega = [0,1]$ and $P$ is uniform. Define $X(\omega) = \omega$ and $Y(\omega) = \omega^2$. Both $X$ and $Y$ are random variables on the same underlying space, but they induce different distributions: $X \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$ while $Y$ has density $p_Y(y) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}}$ for $y \in (0,1)$.

It is common practice to suppress the $\omega$ dependence and write simply $X$, but it is helpful to remember that $X$ is fundamentally a function of the random outcome $\omega$. The probabilities of a random variable are defined as the probability of the random outcome:
$$
P(X \in A) = P(\{\omega \in \Omega \mid X(\omega) \in A\}).
$$
This induces a probability measure $P_X(A) = P(X \in A)$, which is called the *distribution* of $X$, and we write $X \sim P_X$.

**Remark [Notation Convention].**  
We use $P(X \in A)$ and $\mathbb{P}(X \in A)$ interchangeably. Both represent the probability that the random variable $X$ takes a value in the set $A$. The notation $P_X = \text{Law}(X)$ emphasizes that $P_X$ is the distribution induced by $X$.

### Expectation and Moments

#### Definition of Expectation

The expectation of a function $f(X)$ with respect to distribution $P$ is:
$$
\mathbb{E}_{X \sim P}[f(X)] = \int f(x) \,\mathrm{d}P(x) = \int f(x) p(x) \,\mathrm{d}x \quad \text{(continuous)}
$$
$$
\mathbb{E}_{X \sim P}[f(X)] = \sum_x f(x) p(x) \quad \text{(discrete)}.
$$
We use various notations interchangeably: $\mathbb{E}_P[f(X)]$, $\mathbb{E}[f(X)]$, $\mathbb{E}_{X \sim P}[f(X)]$, $\int f \,\mathrm{d}P$, or $\int f(x) P(\mathrm{d}x)$. All represent the same mathematical concept.

#### Key Properties

Expectation satisfies several important properties:

1. **Linearity**: $\mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = a\mathbb{E}[X] + b\mathbb{E}[Y]$ for constants $a,b$
2. **Monotonicity**: If $X \geq Y$ almost surely, then $\mathbb{E}[X] \geq \mathbb{E}[Y]$
3. **Independence**: If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, then $\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$

### Fundamental Theorems

#### Law of Large Numbers

The law of large numbers formalizes what it means for a sequence of random variables to follow a distribution. For independent and identically distributed random variables $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ following distribution $P$, we have:
$$
\frac{f(X_1) + f(X_2) + \cdots + f(X_n)}{n} \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \mathbb{E}_P[f(X)] \quad \text{as } n \to \infty,
$$
for all measurable functions $f$ where $\mathbb{E}_P[f(X)]$ is well-defined. This result justifies using sample averages to estimate population expectations and provides the theoretical foundation for Monte Carlo methods.

**Example [Monte Carlo Integration].**  
To estimate $\mathbb{E}[f(X)]$ where $X \sim P$, we can draw samples $x_1, \ldots, x_n \sim P$ and compute $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i)$. The law of large numbers guarantees this converges to the true expectation.

#### Central Limit Theorem

For independent and identically distributed random variables $X_1, $ $X_2, \ldots, X_n$ with finite mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, the central limit theorem states:
$$
\frac{X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n - n\mu}{\sqrt{n}\sigma} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1).
$$
This fundamental result explains the ubiquity of normal distributions in nature and provides the theoretical foundation for many statistical methods, including confidence intervals and hypothesis testing.

**Remark [Practical Implications].**  
The central limit theorem explains why many natural phenomena follow approximately normal distributions: they arise from the aggregation of many small, independent random effects. This justifies the widespread use of normal approximations in statistics and machine learning.

### Common Distributions

#### Discrete Distributions

- **Bernoulli**: $X \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p)$ with $P(X=1) = p$ and $P(X=0) = 1-p$
- **Binomial**: $X \sim \text{Binomial}(n,p)$ models the number of successes in $n$ independent Bernoulli trials
- **Poisson**: $X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)$ models rare events with rate $\lambda$

#### Continuous Distributions

- **Uniform**: $X \sim \text{Uniform}(a,b)$ has constant density on $[a,b]$
- **Normal**: $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is the most important distribution in statistics
- **Exponential**: $X \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$ models waiting times between events

### Summary and Connections to Machine Learning

Probability theory provides the mathematical language for describing uncertainty in data. The concepts introduced here—random variables, distributions, expectations, and fundamental theorems—form the foundation for:

- **Statistical inference**: Estimating parameters from data
- **Generative modeling**: Learning data distributions
- **Uncertainty quantification**: Measuring confidence in predictions
- **Optimization**: Formulating learning objectives

In the next sections, we will see how these probabilistic concepts are applied to the problem of learning distributions from data through maximum likelihood estimation and other methods. 

### The Issue of Non-measurable Sets

One subtlety of probability on the real domain is that there exist non-measurable sets.   
This involves some deep issues related to the axiom of choice and some of the very foundations of mathematics. 

To give a sense, consider $\mathbb{R}^d$, where it is obvious how to calculate the measure of basic sets, such as rectangles. The idea is to extend the measure to more complex sets. 
Essentially, we can do two things: 

1) If A and B are non-intersecting, then we have $P(A\cup B ) = P(A) + P(B)$.  So $A\cup B$ is measurable. We can repeat this arbitrary number of times (or countable number of times). 

2) If $A$, $B$ is measurable, then $A\setminus B$ is measurable. The complementary is measurable. 

So this is all we can do for calculating the measures. There are no other ways. 

So we can define the set of measurable sets 
as all the sets that can be constructed in this way. 
These sets are called the Borel sets in $\mathbb{R}^d$ when starting from all rectangular sets. 

**Example.**  
We all know the the areas of circles 
are calculated in this way. 

Briefly, in the plane $\mathbb{R}^2$, a cycle can be approximated as the limit of ....

$$ \text{Area} = \lim_{n\to \infty} n r^2 \cos(2\pi/n) = 2 \pi r^2. $$  

**Example [Vitali Set and Axiom of Choice].**  
The Vitali set is a set of real numbers that is not Lebesgue measurable. 

The construction of the Vitali set uses the axiom of choice. 

Specifically, we say that two numbers $x$ and $y$ are equivalent if $x-y$ is a rational number. 
So then the equivalent classes form a partition of $\mathbb{R}$.  
The Vitali set is obtained by picking one element from each of such equivalent classes. 
    
The Vitali set is not Lebesgue measurable.  

**Remark.**  
Assume $A$ is a non-measurable set in $\mathbb{R}^d$. 
Assume $\{x_i\}$  is a sequence of numbers drawing from a random variable $X$ with distribution $P$. 

What happens if we try to calculate limit 
$$
\hat \mu_n(A) := \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{I}(x_i \in A)? 
$$

Well, unfortunately, if $A$ is a non-measurable set, 
it is not that the limit above fails to converge. 
There is a fundamental fault to even talk about it. 

This is because the examples of non-measurable sets 
are constructed by using the axiom of choice, which does not provide a computational procedure to actually construct such sets. Such sets are postulated to exist under the mathematics which assumes the axiom of choice, but there is no way to actually construct them, so there is no way to check if a given number $x_i$ belongs to such a set. 

On the other hand, if $A$ is computable in a proper sense, such that it is possible to check if $x_i \in A$ is true, then $A$ should be a measurable set. 

For example, 
assume that we can check if $x \in A$ of a set of basic 
sets (such as intervals, balls, open and closed sets),
then we can check if $x\in A$ holds for all unions, and complements of such sets. This coincides with the construction of Borel sets. 

if we can check $x_i \in A$, then it means that it can be casted as a countable union and intersection of intervals, then it is a measurable set. 

**Remark.**  
The action of "drawing a realization $x$ from a random variable $X$, or distribution $P_X$" is a problematic concept under the Kolmogorov measure theory. There is no such thing as drawing a realization $x$ from a distribution $X$. 
    
In the Kolmogorov measure theory, the only thing we can say is that let $\{X_i\}$  be a set of i.i.d. random variables with distribution $P$, specifically, it means that the tuple of the random variables follows the product distribution, $(X_i)_{i=1}^n \sim P\times P \cdots \times P$.
     
We can consider another random variable, 
$$
Z = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{I}(X_i \in A). 
$$
Unfortunately, $Z$ is not a measurable function, so it is not a valid random variable, it does not corresponds to a valid probability measure.     

**Remark.**  
In Kolmogorov measure theory, 
there is no such thing as drawing a realization $x$ from a distribution $X$. There is no room for the model of a dynamic random number generator. 

We can calculate areas (measures) of a set.
